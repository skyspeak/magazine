The Casefor aDog

How to ask for a dog so that grown‑ups actually listen. This one is yours. Your grown‑up has their own version, and it is much more boring.

Best if you’re about 8–13 Takes 2 weeks Listen: 6 min Nothing you type is saved online

Start here

Asking again does not work

You have probably already asked. Maybe a lot. And it keeps going the same way, so you ask again, a bit louder, with a slightly sadder face.

Here is the problem. Every time you ask, your grown‑up hears the same thing: a kid who wants something. They stop hearing your actual reasons. It is not because they are mean. It is because asking and proving sound completely different to a grown‑up brain.

What they hear when you ask again“I want it I want it I want it.”
What they hear when you bring proof“This person got up at 6:40 every morning for two weeks and did not once mention it.”

Evidence beats asking. Every single time.

The rest of this is how to get some. It takes about two weeks and it is mostly boring, which is exactly why it works.

Know what you’re up against

They’re not saying no. They’re scared of one specific thing.

Almost every grown‑up who says no to a dog is worried about one of these four. Not all four — usually just one. Your whole job is finding out which.

Tap the one you think it is.

Pick one above

Not sure? That is fine — go and ask them, then come back. Asking “what worries you most about it?” is not a trick. It is just a good question.

Then ask them this exact sentence

“What would have to be true for this to be a yes?”

This question is the best one in the whole zine, and here is why it works. It is not a fight. It does not ask them to say yes today. It asks them to describe the yes — and once they describe it, you know exactly what to go and do.

Most kids never find that out. They just keep asking and hoping something changes.

Then do the hardest part

Be quiet and listen to the entire answer. Do not argue with any of it. Not one bit. You are collecting information, and you cannot collect it while you are talking.

Write it down in front of them

Actually get a pen. It feels a bit silly and it works anyway, because it shows you are treating their answer as real instead of as an obstacle.

The evidence

Five missions

Do these in order. Number 2 is the one that actually changes minds, and it is the one almost nobody finishes.

1

Find out which of the four it is

Ask, listen, write it down. You cannot fix a worry you have not identified — and if you guess wrong, you will spend two weeks proving something they were never worried about.

One conversation
2

Fourteen mornings

Get yourself up before anyone calls you, and do one real job at the same time every day. Take the bins out. Walk a neighbour’s dog. Feed a pet you already have. Make your own breakfast and clean it up.

Fourteen days in a row. And here is the trick: do not talk about it. Do not point it out. Let them notice on their own, because a thing they noticed themselves is worth about ten times a thing you told them.

Tap a circle each morning you do it.

Missed a day? Do not quit. Start again and tell them you are starting again. That is honestly more impressive than a perfect streak, because perfect streaks are easy to fake and admitting a restart is not.

14 days · the important one
3

Find the real number

A dog costs much more than the price of the dog. Food, vet visits, medicine, a licence, and one big surprise vet bill that most dogs have at some point in their life.

Ask your grown‑up to open Worksheet A in their version of this magazine — it works out the real cost for your family, for your kind of dog, in your town. Do it together. Sitting next to someone while they add up a scary number is a completely different thing from being told the number.

20 minutes, together
4

Name three things you’ll give up

“Some screen time” is not a real thing. A real thing has a name and a day attached to it.

Not this

“I’ll do less gaming.”
“I’ll be more helpful.”
“I’ll do everything!”

This

“Saturday mornings, which is when I usually do nothing.”
“Half my Friday club.”
“Sleeping in on weekends. All of them.”

Anyone can want a dog. Giving something real up is what turns a wish into a decision — and grown‑ups can spot the difference instantly.

Write all three down
5

Argue against yourself

This is the move that actually wins, and nobody believes it the first time they hear it.

Go to your grown‑up and say: “Here is the best reason NOT to get a dog right now.” Then say a real one. A good one. The one they are actually thinking about at night.

The second you do that, you stop being a kid who wants something and you become a person they can have a conversation with. It is the most grown‑up thing you can do in this entire process, and it works on everything for the rest of your life. Not just dogs.

The one that wins

Words

Say this, not that

Makes it worse

  • “I’ll do EVERYTHING, I promise!”
  • “Maya’s mum let her have one.”
  • “You never let me have anything.”
  • “It’s not even that expensive.”
  • “Fine. Forget it.” (door)

Makes them listen

  • “I’ll do the morning walk on school days.”
  • “I looked up what it costs. Can I show you?”
  • “What would have to be true for this to be a yes?”
  • “Here’s the best reason not to.”
  • “Okay. When can we talk about it again?”

See the pattern? The left column is about you. The right column is about the dog and the plan. Promising everything sounds like the biggest promise you can make, but grown‑ups hear it as the least believable one, because they know nobody does everything.

One small promise you actually keep beats a huge one you don’t. Every time.

Ammunition

Four true things that make you sound like you know what you’re talking about

Because you will. These are real, and most kids asking for a dog have no idea about any of them.

A dog is about 2 hours of work a day

Walks, food, cleaning up, training. Every day. Including when you are ill, on your birthday, and when it is raining sideways in February.

Puppies are the hard version

A puppy wakes up in the night for weeks and chews everything you own. A dog that is already three or four is often calmer, already house‑trained, and shelters are completely full of them. Saying “I think we should get an older dog” will genuinely surprise your grown‑up.

Ten minutes of training. Every day. For a year.

That is the difference between a dog you love living with and a dog that drags you down the street. Not a magic trick — just ten boring minutes, over and over.

A bored dog chews things

It is not being bad. It is being bored. Nearly every “bad dog” is actually a bored dog whose brain has nothing to do.

The honest bit

A sad thing you should probably know now

Dogs do not live as long as people. Usually about twelve years.

So a dog you got this year would be with you for the rest of school, and then you would lose him, and that would really hurt.

Almost everyone who has had a dog will tell you the same thing anyway: completely worth it. But it is not a secret, and it is not a reason to say no, and you are old enough to know it before you decide rather than after.

Go and talk to your grown‑up about this part

Not because it is scary. Because things like this are much easier out loud than sitting quietly in your head. Ask them how long dogs live. Ask if they ever had one. That second question usually gets you a story.

If it doesn’t go your way

“No” and “not yet” are different answers

They feel identical in your stomach. They are not the same thing at all.

If it’s “not yet”

Ask two things. What has to change? And when will we talk about it again? Then write the date down — and do not bring it up until then.

That last part is really hard. It is also the exact thing that makes them take you seriously next time.

If it’s a real no

How you take a no is part of your case. Not for the dog — for the next thing. And there is always a next thing.

A kid who hears no, says “okay, thanks for actually explaining it,” and does not slam anything is a kid who gets a much more serious hearing next time. That is not a trick. It is just true.

You can still be around dogs

Walk one

A neighbour with a dog and a busy week is often thrilled. Sometimes it even pays.

Ask about fostering

Fostering means looking after a rescue dog at your house until it finds a family. Rescues are always short of foster homes. Ask your grown‑up to phone one.

Volunteer, later

Lots of shelters take helpers from about twelve, and older for handling the dogs. Ring and ask what their age is.

Be a dog‑borrowing family

Someone else’s dog every other weekend is a genuinely great life, and it costs almost nothing.

Put it together

Build your one page

Fill this in once you have done the missions. It makes the page you actually hand over — on paper if you can, because paper is harder to ignore than a phone screen.

Size, age, energy. Remember mission 3 — an older dog is a strong answer.

One job. Named. With a time on it.

A real one. The one they actually think about.

Asking for a conversation on a date is much easier to say yes to than asking for a dog.
