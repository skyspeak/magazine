Issue No. 08 · Usually 7–14

So you want your own money

The decision is not how much. It is whether you are giving them money, or giving them the right to make a bad purchase - because only the second one teaches anything.

What you are actually deciding

Start here, because the wrong question wastes the conversation

The real question

Not what is the right amount. It is: am I actually going to let them lose it?

  • The amount, and how often
  • Whether it is tied to chores
  • Whether you keep a veto

Facts worth knowing

Money only teaches when it can be lost. An allowance that gets topped up after a bad decision is a lesson that has been deleted, at full price.

Pricing every household job creates a market where there was a family. Once the bins have a rate, carrying your own plate becomes negotiable.

How much, at what age?Nothing here is saved or sent

There is no correct number. This is a common range and what it tends to buy.

Say this, not that

Adapt the words, keep the structure

Say

It is yours. I will tell you once if I think something is a bad idea, and then I will stop. And I am not going to replace it. That is the whole point of it being yours.

States the deal and pre-commits you to the hard part, in front of a witness.

After the inevitable bad purchase

Yeah. That is a rubbish one, isn’t it. What will you do differently with the next lot?

No lecture, no rescue, no I-told-you-so. The disappointment is doing the teaching; do not interrupt it.

Not this

Everything is paid, per chore.

It prices things that should be free, and you will eventually find yourself negotiating a fee with a child for tidying their own room.

Let them buy the rubbish thing.

A twenty-pound regret at nine is the cheapest financial education available anywhere, and it does not go on a credit record.

The longer version

If you have ten minutes rather than two

Tie it to chores, or not?

The tidy answer: some jobs are family membership and are unpaid - your own plate, your own room, your own washing. Extra jobs beyond that can be paid, at a rate agreed in advance.

That split gives you the lesson of earning without turning the household into a marketplace. It also means the allowance itself can be unconditional, which matters, because an allowance you can withdraw is not really theirs and does not teach ownership.

Saving, without making it joyless

Splitting money into spend, save and give sounds excellent and mostly fails, because the proportions get imposed. A simpler version works better: they spend what they like, and anything for something big goes in a visible jar or a named account.

The visible part matters. A number in your banking app is abstract; a jar filling up is not. And when they finally buy the thing, be pleased about it - a save that ends in a lecture about whether it was worth it teaches them not to save.

If it is not yet

A smaller amount, weekly, in cash, where they can physically watch it leave. Cash is a better teacher than a card at this age precisely because it visibly runs out.
