Issue No. 05 · Usually 8–12

So you want to go on your own

Independence is not a switch you flip at an age. It is a distance, and you extend it. The question is never the world in general - it is this route, at this hour, with this child.

What you are actually deciding

Start here, because the wrong question wastes the conversation

The real question

Not is the world safe. It is: does my kid have the judgement for this specific journey, and do they know what to do when it goes wrong?

  • Which route, at what time
  • What happens when plan A fails
  • How they tell you they arrived

Facts worth knowing

The risk on a walk to school is overwhelmingly traffic, not strangers. Rehearse the road, not the fear.

A long warning about strangers reliably raises anxiety without raising a single skill. Twenty minutes practising the worst junction does the opposite.

Together, on the real route, at the real time.

Say this, not that

Adapt the words, keep the structure

Say

We are going to walk it together three times. Then you walk it while I wait at the other end. Then you do it on your own. And before any of that - tell me what you would do if the shop is shut.

A ladder with a named top rung, and a failure question rather than a fear lecture.

Instead of the stranger talk

If you ever need help and I am not there, ask a woman with children, or someone working in a shop.

Actionable. ‘Don’t talk to strangers’ leaves a lost child with no one to ask.

Not this

Nothing is going to happen.

It is a promise you cannot keep, and it teaches them that surprises are outside the plan rather than part of it. Then the first surprise feels like catastrophe.

Name the failure plan out loud before the first solo trip. Shop shut, friend not there, phone dead, feels wrong.

Confidence comes from having somewhere to go when the plan breaks - not from being told it will not break.

The longer version

If you have ten minutes rather than two

Build the ladder, not the leap

Together, three times, at the real time of day. Then they walk while you wait at the far end. Then they walk and you are at home. Then a different route.

Each rung is small enough to be boring, which is exactly why it works. Nobody's judgement improves by being thrown into the deep end; it improves by being right about small things repeatedly.

What to give them, and what not to

Give them: the route, the failure plan, a memorised phone number, and a way to say they have arrived.

Do not give them: a running commentary of everything that could go wrong. A child who has been frightened into independence is not independent, they are just alone and scared.

If it is not yet

Shorten the leg. Half the route, or the same route at a busier hour, is a genuine yes rather than a soft no - and it is the next rung, not a consolation prize.
