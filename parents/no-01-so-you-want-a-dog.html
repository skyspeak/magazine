FROM THE EDITORS
Why we started with the dog

The practice round

A child asks for a dog with a face they have practiced in a mirror. It is easy to treat this as noise , a want, like a want for gum , and to answer it the way we answer noise: we’ll see.

But look at what they are doing. They are gathering evidence. They are anticipating your objections. They are proposing terms, absorbing a no, and coming back with a revised offer. Somewhere between seven and twelve, the dog question is the first time most kids build a case for something that costs real money and changes the shape of a household.

That makes it a rehearsal. Behind it, in order, come the phone, the sleepover, the curfew, the car, the job, the year off, the person they want to move in with. Every one of those asks will be answered by a process your kid has already learned from you , and they learn it here, on the dog.

So this issue is not really pro‑dog or anti‑dog. It is built to make the decision legible: to put the real number on the table, to name who does the six a.m. walk in February, to preview the ending, and to write the whole thing down on one page that everybody signs.

Do that, and you can get any of the three honest answers , yes, not yet, or no , without the relationship taking damage. That is the actual skill. The dog is just how you practise it.

Read this issue with your kid, not about them.

Do not use the number as a weapon.The point is not to win the argument. The point is to hand them a real trade.

There is a version of this page where a parent says “do you have any idea what a dog costs?” and walks away feeling like they scored a point. That version teaches nothing except that money is a conversation‑ender.

Do the opposite. Put the calculator in front of them and let them turn the dials. A nine‑year‑old cannot feel twenty‑eight thousand dollars . That number is weather. But a nine‑year‑old can absolutely feel this is four family holidays and we are choosing the dog instead. That is a real trade, and kids are far better at holding real trades than we give them credit for. They just almost never get shown one.

Three cost traps that catch good families

1 : The haircut

You fall for a curly‑coated dog at the shelter. Nobody mentions that a curly coat is a standing appointment every six to eight weeks for the next twelve years. Set the coat dial to curly and watch what it does.

2 : The sock

Most dogs have one financial emergency in a lifetime. A swallowed sock that needs surgery is roughly $2,500–$5,000. A torn knee ligament, very common in bigger dogs, is $3,000–$6,000. Decide now what you would do at that number, not in the waiting room.

3 : The second job

Daycare and boarding are the line that quietly doubles a budget. If nobody is home for nine hours a day, that is not a scheduling detail. It is the single most expensive thing about your dog.

If the dog is only a good idea when your child does the work, it is not a good idea.The decision rule for this entire issue

Here is the honest planning assumption, and it is not cynicism, it is just what happens: the adult does about eighty per cent of the work. Not because your kid is lazy. Because they are nine, and school happens, and football happens, and eighty per cent of dog labour occurs at hours when a child is either asleep or being driven somewhere.

Design the household around that number and everyone is fine. Design it around a promise a nine‑year‑old made in a moment of longing, and in eighteen months you will be standing in the rain at half six, quietly furious at a child for being a child.

So get the dog because your family wants a dog. Whatever your kid contributes on top of that is a bonus , and you should treat it, loudly, like one.

The thing the brochures leave out

The part nobody previews

We are going to say this plainly, because most articles about children and dogs will not. If you get a dog for a seven‑year‑old, that dog will most likely die when your child is about nineteen.

It will probably be the first death they experience up close. There is a decent chance they will not be in the house when it happens . That they will take the call in a corridor at university, or on a break at a first job, and have to walk back into a room and be normal.

This is not an argument against the dog. Honestly, it is one of the better arguments for it. Learning that love runs on a clock , in the safest possible container, at an age when your whole family is still standing around you , is not a small thing to be handed. Most people learn it much later and much worse.

But you should know that you are buying it. It is in the box, at the original price, and it is not an optional extra.

Dogs live about twelve years. Say the sentence once, at the beginning, and then go and pick the dog.What to say , and then stop saying

You, once, early, not solemnlyDogs live about twelve years. So this one will be here until you’re nearly finished with school, and then we’ll lose him, and it will be really sad, and it will still have been completely worth it.

Say it once. Do not repeat it, do not turn it into a lesson, and do not let it hang over the first walk. Children can hold a fact like this far better than adults expect, provided it is delivered as a fact and not as a warning.

Four other endings, less final, more common

The dog that doesn’t like children

It happens, including with dogs who tested fine. Ask the rescue directly what their return policy is and how they assess kid‑safety, and treat any answer that sounds breezy as a red flag. A bite in the house is an emergency, not a training problem.

The allergy that shows up at month four

Before you commit, spend real hours , several visits, indoors, over weeks , with the type of dog you are considering. No breed is truly hypoallergenic. Finding out afterwards means choosing between a child’s asthma and a family member.

The move

Jobs change, relationships change, landlords change. Ask out loud: if we had to move in three years, is the dog a constraint we accept on where we live? If the honest answer is no, you have your answer about the dog.

The kid who stops caring

Around thirteen, the dog often stops being a project and becomes furniture. This is normal development, not betrayal, and it is the single most common source of household resentment. See the agreement on p.7, and the voicemail in the podcast.

Scripts, by age

What to say

“We’ll see” is not a parenting strategy. It is a delaying tactic, children can smell it from another room, and it teaches them that the way to move a parent is volume and repetition rather than argument.

Here is what to say instead. Adapt the words; keep the structure.

Ages 6 to 8 : the dog is yours, the job is theirs

At this age a child cannot be a caretaker, and it is unkind to let them believe otherwise. What they can do is one small, visible, daily job that is theirs and matters.

SayThis is going to be the family’s dog, and taking care of him is mostly my job, because he’s a lot of work and you’re seven. Your job is the water bowl. Every single day, fresh water. That’s a real job and he needs you to do it.

Don’t sayIf we get a dog, you have to promise to take care of him.

Why: a promise a seven‑year‑old cannot keep is a debt you will collect on for a decade.

Ages 9 to 12 . Make them build the case

This is the sweet spot, and the whole reason this issue exists. Nine to twelve is old enough to research, budget, anticipate objections and negotiate , and young enough to still want to.

So do not decide in your head and announce it. Ask for a proposal, in writing, on one page. Give them the brief:

The briefI’m taking this seriously, so I want it in writing. One page. What it costs to start and every year after. What kind of dog and why that kind. What happens on football days. What happens when we go to Grandma’s for a week. And what you’re giving up to make room for it. Take a week. Then we’ll sit down properly and I’ll give you a real answer.

That last item is the entire ballgame. What are you giving up is the question that separates a want from a decision, and it is the one question kids are almost never asked. Then hold up your end: read it properly, take notes, and answer the arguments they made.

Hand them the Job Application on p.6 as the starting form.

Ages 13 and up . Be straight about the clock

Here the arithmetic changes and you should say so. A dog acquired for a fifteen‑year‑old will have nine or ten years left when that fifteen‑year‑old moves out.

SayI want to be straight with you. In four years you’ll probably be gone, and I’ll have a dog for another eight. So if we do this, I’m mostly getting this dog for me. I’m okay with that , but I’m not going to pretend it’s yours.

Teenagers respond well to being told the true structure of a deal. What they cannot stand is a deal that pretends to be theirs and is quietly not.

The three legitimate answers

Answer 1Yes

Say it warmly and completely. No conditions bolted on afterwards, no “but if you don’t walk him…”. Then go straight to the agreement on p.7 while everyone is happy . That is the easiest time to write down who does mornings.

Answer 2Not yet

The answer most families need. It only works with a reason, a condition and a date. Without a date it is a no wearing a costume, and children see straight through the costume.

Answer 3No

Sometimes it is a no, and a clean no is kinder than a decade of hedging. Give the real reason, do not dress it up as their fault, and offer something concrete instead . See below.

How “not yet” should sound

SayNot this year. Here’s the real reason: with both of us out of the house until six, a dog would be alone for nine hours a day and that isn’t fair to him. Here’s what has to be true : one of us working from home two days a week, or the daycare money in the budget. We’ll sit down again in March and I’ll show you where we are. I’m putting it in the calendar now.

Then put it in the calendar, in front of them, and keep it. What your child is really learning in this conversation is not whether they get a dog. It is whether the process in this house is honest . Whether a well‑made argument gets a real hearing, and whether a promise from a parent survives four months of nothing happening.

That lesson gets cashed later, at the curfew conversation, at three in the morning, when you need them to believe that what you say is what will happen.

If the answer is no, offer one of these instead

  • Foster. Local rescues are chronically short of foster homes, they usually pay for food and vet care, and it is dogs with an end date.
  • A paid dog‑walking round. Two neighbours, four afternoons a week. Real money, real responsibility, and it settles the “I would definitely walk him” question permanently.
  • Shelter volunteering, usually from around twelve, often sixteen for handling . Call and ask.
  • Be a dog‑borrowing family. A standing arrangement with a friend’s dog every other weekend is a good life and about four per cent of the cost.

Protocol & the one rule

The two‑week trial

Every family in this situation is trying to predict how they will feel about a thing they have never done. There is a cheap, obvious fix that almost nobody uses: borrow a dog first.

Foster through a local rescue . They will nearly always say yes, and they usually cover food and veterinary costs. Or dog‑sit for a neighbour. Or, at minimum, take the neighbour’s dog out at half six every morning for fourteen days running.

Fourteen days, not a weekend. A weekend is a holiday. Two weeks is where the novelty burns off, where it rains twice, where somebody gets sick, and where you find out who in this house gets up.

Never make the decision in front of the dog.The one rule , the rest of this issue depends on it

Do not walk into a shelter undecided. Do not go “just to look.” Because you will meet a two‑year‑old shepherd mix with one folded ear who rests his chin on your child’s knee, and from that moment you are not deciding anything . You are doing paperwork with wet eyes and a story about fate.

Decide at your kitchen table, cold. Write down the size, the age, the energy level, the coat, the ceiling on the budget, and the name of the human doing mornings. Take the page with you. Then go and meet dogs, and only consider the ones that fit what you wrote.

Decide before you go

  • Adult dog or puppy , and be honest about house‑training at night
  • Weight ceiling, in kilos or pounds, written down
  • Energy: a working breed needs a job, not a garden
  • Coat, because the coat is a standing bill
  • Monthly ceiling from Worksheet A

Ask the shelter, out loud

  • What is your return policy, and how often is it used?
  • How has this dog been assessed around children?
  • What is known about the last home, and why did it end?
  • What medical history are you aware of?
  • Can we do a home visit and a second meeting a week apart?

An adult dog, three to six years old, is the single most under‑rated choice a family with children can make. They are usually house‑trained, their size and temperament are known rather than hoped for, they sleep through the night, and shelters cannot rehome them fast enough. The puppy is for the parents. The four‑year‑old is for the family.

The Family Agreement

Whatever you decide, write it down while everyone is still happy. An agreement made on the good day is the thing that saves you on the bad one.

The point is not to have something to wave at a teenager. The point is that a vague deal collapses silently, and nobody can tell whether it was broken , which is precisely how resentment gets in.

4. The clause that is not negotiable

The dog is never used as leverage. Nobody in this house threatens to give the dog away, rehome him, or “send him back” to enforce a chore, a grade or a mood , not once, not as a joke. Take the screen. Take the weekend. The dog is off the table.

Why it matters: the moment the dog becomes a bargaining chip, he stops being safe , and the lesson your child absorbs is that belonging in this family is conditional on performance.

5. Review

Treat this page as a working document rather than a verdict. Put the first review three months out, in the calendar, now. At the review you are not asking “has everyone been good.” You are asking “is this page still true?” , and if it is not, you rewrite the page rather than blaming the people.
