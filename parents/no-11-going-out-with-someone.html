Issue No. 11 · Usually 13–17

So you want to go out with someone

This one arrives whether or not you decided anything. So the real decision is not permission. It is whether they will tell you anything once it has started.

What you are actually deciding

Start here, because the wrong question wastes the conversation

The real question

Not should I allow this. It is: am I someone they can report to without a scene?

  • What ‘going out’ is allowed to mean right now, specifically
  • Where, and with how many other people around
  • What you actually need to know, as opposed to want to know

Facts worth knowing

The information channel is worth more than any rule you can set. Children who expect a calm reaction tell their parents things; children who expect a scene stop reporting, and you find out much later and from someone else.

Your face in the first four seconds decides whether you get told the next thing. Not your policy - your face.

They just told you something. What is your face doing?Nothing here is saved or sent

Pick the one you would actually get. The right answer is the same shape every time.

Say this, not that

Adapt the words, keep the structure

Say

I would like to meet them. Not to interrogate anybody - I just want a face to go with the name. And I promise not to make it weird.

Low stakes, specific, and it names the fear they actually have, which is that you will make it weird.

The sentence that matters most

If anything ever happens that you are not okay with, you can tell me and I will not be angry with you. Not ever, not about that.

Say it once, early, plainly. It is the single most important sentence in this issue and it has to be in place before it is needed.

Not this

Ooooh, is this your girlfriend?

Teasing at fourteen is cheap and closes the channel for years. It is the most expensive joke available to a parent.

Stay unshockable. React in the morning, not in the moment.

You can have any opinion you like, later. What you cannot do is have it on your face in the first four seconds.

The longer version

If you have ten minutes rather than two

Set the shape, not the rules

At thirteen or fourteen, ‘going out’ often means messaging and sitting together at break. Rules built for something more than that are rules about a thing that is not happening, and they read as an accusation.

Ask what it actually means before you legislate. Then agree the shape - group settings, public places, home by a time - rather than an inventory of prohibitions you cannot observe anyway.

The conversation nobody wants to have

Consent, pressure, and what to do when something is not okay. It belongs here, it belongs early, and it belongs in plain language rather than euphemism.

Keep it short and repeat it rarely: nobody is entitled to anything from you, you can stop at any point, and you can ring me from anywhere at any hour and I will come and I will not be angry. Then be someone who would actually do that.

How you will know

If it is not yet

Be specific instead of vague: what is allowed now, what is allowed at sixteen, and when you will talk about it again. ‘We’ll see’ is heard as no, and it costs you the conversation.
